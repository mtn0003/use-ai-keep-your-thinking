Know the line

Nobody is quite sure what is allowed

Nine of the ten students interviewed knew a rule existed. Not one could say what it actually let them do. Here is the shortest useful version.

First, find your level

Many universities set each assessment at one of five levels on the AI Assessment Scale. The level is chosen by whoever set the work, so it changes from one assignment to the next. Find that first, because it decides everything else.

LevelWhat it allows
No AIDone entirely without AI, in controlled conditions.
AI PlanningAI for preparation only: brainstorming, outlining, early research. What you hand in is developed and written by you.
AI CollaborationAI may help you complete the task, including drafting and feedback, but you must judge and change what it produces.
Full AIAI may be used for any part, with you directing it.
AI ExplorationAI used creatively to solve problems, by design.

Level names quoted from the University of Stirling, Learning and Teaching Enhancement, "AI Assessment Scale". The three bands below are a plain-language illustration, not a substitute.

The bits people actually get stuck on

Saying what you used

One student summed the problem up: "I find it so confusing, all the citations." The rule itself is short. Be honest about any use, acknowledge it, and check what your own module asks for, because a module policy beats any general guide.

Where the acknowledgement goes depends on your referencing style. In IEEE, which many computing courses use, AI is currently acknowledged in a footnote rather than in the reference list. Other styles may want it in the reference list, or the output itself in an appendix.

Text generated by [name of AI], [developer], [month day, year], [URL].

Pattern from the University of Stirling referencing guide. Example: Text generated by ChatGPT, OpenAI, March 7, 2023, https://chat.openai.com/chat.

Telling the difference

Spotting it is harder than people think

In one published experiment, 210 people watching a mix of real and fake videos were right 57.6% of the time, which is barely better than guessing. The people most confident in their own judgement did worst of all.

The short version. Stop staring at the thing and start asking where it came from. Who published it, does anyone else report it, does the account have any history, and does the source say what it is claimed to say.

N. C. Köbis, B. Doležalová and I. Soraperra, "Fooled twice: People cannot detect deepfakes but think they can," iScience, vol. 24, no. 11, 103364, 2021.

Quick check

Five questions

Nothing here is recorded or sent anywhere. The explanations are the useful part.

1. Checking a source

You ask a tool a factual question. It answers, and adds a source. What is the safest next thing to do?

These tools produce sources that do not exist. Worse, they can produce a plausible looking website, so finding that a link works is not the same as finding it is trustworthy. Asking the same tool to check itself does not help, because the same process produced the error.

2. Confident writing

An answer comes back fluent, confident and well organised. What does that tell you about whether it is correct?

Fluency and accuracy come out of the same process, so one tells you nothing about the other. As one student put it: "if it's wrong, it will still say it with confidence, as if it was right."

3. Spotting a generated video

Which of these is a reliable way to tell whether a video was AI generated?

All three are giveaways that are disappearing, and none survives on its own. What works better is asking where it came from, whether anyone else is reporting it, and whether the account posting it has any history.

4. Saying you used it

You asked a tool to suggest a structure for your essay. Every word you handed in was yours. Do you say so?

Acknowledge any use, then check where your referencing style wants the acknowledgement to go. Declaring costs you nothing. Not declaring is the thing that carries a risk.

5. Over the line

Which of these is most clearly over the line at most universities?

The other three use the tool to understand something, and the words you submit stay yours. One student offered a test that is easier to remember than any policy: you should be able to explain what you handed in.
